## 5 · Why exactly seven

<Callout emoji="✍️">
**Your prose here.**

*Verified facts to write against:* stepping up a third is `+2`, and
`gcd(2, 7) = 1`, so `+2` cycles through all seven letters before repeating.
Bottom lines go **G B D F A C E**, then back to G. Returning to the starting
letter takes 7 steps of 2 = +14 diatonic steps, which is exactly **two
octaves** — so an eighth stop is bass clef displaced, not a new clef.
</Callout>

## 6 · Why only three symbols

<Callout emoji="✍️">
**Your prose here.**

*Verified facts to write against:* the F clef points at **F3**, the C clef at
**middle C**, the G clef at **G4**. Four diatonic steps each way — a perfect
fifth below middle C and a perfect fifth above. Perfectly symmetric. Three
symbols because three named notes. The seven clefs split **2 F / 4 C / 1 G**.
</Callout>

## 7 · The seven clefs, and their real symbols

<Callout emoji="✍️">
**Your prose here.** These are the seven clefs history actually used, and here

*Verified facts:* only four survive — **treble, bass, alto, tenor**. Obsolete:
**baritone, mezzo-soprano, soprano**.

## 8 · Who still reads alto and tenor

<Callout emoji="✍️">
**Your prose here.**

*Verified facts to write against:*
- **Alto** — viola, essentially its only full-time user.
- **Tenor** — *no* full-time user at all. It is a register-saving clef that
  cello, bassoon, trombone, euphonium and double bass switch *into* when the
  bass staff would need too many ledger lines.
- The conventional clef is a ledger-line minimum for **10 of 13** instruments
  in the widget below. The three exceptions: guitar (treble clef is an
  octave-transposing convention), trombone (tenor genuinely wins up high),
  alto voice (historically alto clef, moved to treble).
</Callout>
