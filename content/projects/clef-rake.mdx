{/* ── 5 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  PROSE: Why exactly seven.
  FACT: stepping up a third is +2; gcd(2,7) = 1, so +2 cycles through all
  seven letters before repeating. Bottom lines go G B D F A C E, then back
  to G. Returning to the start takes 7 steps of 2 = +14 = TWO octaves, so
  the eighth stop is bass clef displaced, not a new clef.
*/}

{/* ── 6 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  PROSE: Why only three symbols.
  FACT: F clef points at F3, C clef at middle C, G clef at G4. Four
  diatonic steps each way—a perfect fifth below and a perfect fifth above
  middle C. Perfectly symmetric. Three symbols because three named notes.
  The seven split 2 F / 4 C / 1 G.
*/}

{/* ── 7 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  PROSE: These are the seven clefs history actually used, and here are
  their real symbols—the markers were the letters F, C and G all along.
  Only four survive: treble, bass, alto, tenor.
  Obsolete: baritone, mezzo-soprano, soprano.
*/}

{/* ── 8 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  PROSE: Who still reads alto and tenor, and the ledger-line reason.
  FACTS:
    - Alto: viola, essentially its only full-time user.
    - Tenor: NO full-time user at all. It is a register-saving clef that
      cello, bassoon, trombone, euphonium and double bass switch INTO when
      the bass staff would need too many ledger lines.
    - The conventional clef is a ledger-line minimum for 10 of 13
      instruments below. The exceptions: guitar (treble clef is an
      octave-transposing convention), trombone (tenor genuinely wins up
      high), alto voice (historically alto clef, moved to treble).
*/}
