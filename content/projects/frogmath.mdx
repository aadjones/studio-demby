## The Problem

Most students encounter number theory as a wall of abstract symbols—integers, divisibility, GCD—completely divorced from physical intuition. They memorize the Euclidean algorithm without understanding *why* it works. They calculate linear combinations mechanically, never seeing the elegant structure beneath.

**FrogMath** grounds these concepts in a spatial, interactive model that students can see and manipulate before they ever encounter formal notation.

## The Core Metaphor

Instead of asking "Does 3 divide 12?", we ask: **"Can a frog that hops in intervals of 3 reach lily pad 12?"**

The answer is the same, but the question now has spatial meaning. Students can *see* the pattern of where a 3-hopper can land. They build intuition through exploration before encountering formal definitions.

## Bridging to Formal Mathematics

The real pedagogical power comes from the **Math Concepts** reference sheet, which explicitly connects the informal frog language to standard mathematical notation:

<div className="my-8">
  <img
    src="/photos/frogmath/concepts.jpg"
    alt="Math Concepts reference sheet showing mappings between FrogMath and standard notation"
    className="w-full rounded-lg shadow-lg border-2 border-green-200"
  />
</div>

Students learn that:
- **"The river"** = The set of integers (ℤ)
- **"The fly set"** = The set of multiples of a hopper's jump size (formally, the ideal aℤ)
- **"Alice mimics Bob"** = Alice's fly set contains Bob's fly set (equivalently, Alice's hop divides Bob's hop)
- **"Hop combos"** = Linear combinations

This isn't just metaphor—it's a genuine conceptual scaffold that transfers to formal mathematics.

## What Works (and What Doesn't)

### Strengths

**Spatial intuition is powerful.** Students report that the frog model is "sticky" and memorable. Seeing that a 6-hopper can reach anywhere a 3-hopper can reach (because 3 divides 6) creates lasting understanding.

**Progression from play to proof.** Students explore freely in Pond mode, solve structured problems in Multi-hopper, then see the formal mathematics in the Concepts sheet. This matches how mathematical understanding actually develops.

**The Euclidean algorithm becomes visible.** Finding which pads two hoppers can both reach naturally leads to questions about GCD. Students discover the algorithm before being told it.

### Limitations

**Some concepts don't map naturally.** Prime factorization and modular arithmetic are harder to represent with hopping frogs. FrogMath works best as an *introduction* to number theory, not a complete curriculum.

**The metaphor creates scaffolding debt.** At some point, students need to work abstractly with symbols. There's pedagogical tension between making math accessible and ensuring students can eventually transcend the concrete model.

**It's not a substitute for proof.** FrogMath builds intuition, but students still need to learn rigorous argumentation. The spatial model can sometimes make things *seem* more obvious than they are, potentially undermining the need for proof.

## Design Philosophy

Every design choice serves the learning goal:

- **Constrained exploration before open play**: Single-hopper mode introduces one concept at a time before Pond mode opens everything up
- **Visual clarity over realism**: The lily pads are arranged in a circle for visibility, not ecological accuracy
- **Problems provoke discovery**: Multi-hopper challenges are designed to make students notice patterns, not drill procedures
- **Terminology builds bridges**: "Mimicry" and "hop combos" feel playful but map directly to divisibility and linear combinations

## Technical Implementation

Built with vanilla JavaScript and SVG manipulation for responsive, accessible interaction. The entire app runs client-side with no backend—perfect for classroom use with unreliable internet.

The hopping animations use CSS transforms for smooth, 60fps movement across different devices. Touch and mouse events are handled uniformly so the experience works identically on tablets and desktops.

## Try It Yourself

FrogMath is free and open-source. Try it with your students, or just hop around yourself to rediscover number theory through a frog's eyes.
