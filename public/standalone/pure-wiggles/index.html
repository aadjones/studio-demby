Pure Wiggles

Good vibes only...but what are vibrations, actually? Let's find out.

Chapter 1

What is a vibration?

Pluck a guitar string and freeze time the instant after you release it. You've made a disturbance in the string. The string is displeased. Tension pulls every point of the string back toward its desirable resting state of a flat line. The more of a disturbance you make, the harder the string pulls back.

Let's let time continue after you pluck that string. Is the string now vibrating? In casual terms, sure. But if you film that plucked string in slow motion, you'll see its shape writhe: the disturbance bounces from end to end. A clean vibration (good vibes only) would be something more strict: every point moving in lockstep, all rising together, all falling together. These good vibrations are called modes of vibration or standing waves.

So how can we get these glorious modes of vibration? First, let's get the string into mathematics, where we can interrogate it. Freeze it again and look at what the camera caught: a curve. Lay a ruler along the resting line and call position along it x. At every position, the frozen string sits some height above or below rest—call that height f(x). That's the whole dictionary: a snapshot of a string is a function, and the graph of the function is the string. (Notice there's no time variable—this chapter lives entirely inside one frozen frame.)

The frozen string, as mathematics: at every position x, one number f(x)—how far that point has strayed from rest. The nailed-down ends force f = 0 there. Remember the nails; they get a vote later.

Next, the physics. We said the string pulls back harder where you disturb it more—but what exactly does it measure when deciding how hard? Zoom in on one little piece of string. Its neighbors tug it from both sides, along the string itself: that's all tension is. If the piece is straight, the two tugs point in exactly opposite directions and cancel—no net force, no complaint. If the piece is bent, the tugs miss each other slightly, and the miss leaves a net pull aimed at flattening the bend—the tighter the bend, the bigger the leftover. So the string doesn't care how far you've displaced a point; it cares how bent the string is there. The pull-back is proportional to the curvature—and the curvature of a graph is something calculus already owns: the second derivative, f″. Newton then says that leftover pull is what accelerates the piece. (Chapter 3 will write this as an honest equation and let time run. For now, the picture is all we need.)

One piece of string under the magnifying glass. Tension tugs it from both ends, along the string. Straight piece: the tugs cancel exactly. Bent piece: they don't, and the leftover pull points toward straightness (the dashed line)—harder where the bend is tighter. That leftover, mapped point by point, is the second derivative f″.

Now aim the physics at the goal. Lockstep means the shape never changes—it only breathes: every later snapshot is the same curve, rescaled. A point displaced twice as far as its neighbor now must stay exactly twice as far for the whole ride. And what steers the ride is acceleration—so the accelerations must come in those same proportions too: every point yanked in exact proportion to how far it strayed, one common ratio for the entire string. But we just learned what the yank is: curvature. Put the two sentences together and the requirement writes itself—the curvature must be proportional to the displacement itself, the same ratio everywhere, with a minus sign so the pull is a pull-back:

f″ = −(number) · f WANTED: CURVATURE EVERYWHERE PROPORTIONAL TO DISPLACEMENT, AND OPPOSED TO IT

What lockstep demands, drawn: each snapshot is the previous one rescaled, so every displacement arrow shrinks (or grows, or flips) by one common factor. This is the audition requirement, not a winner—whether any shape can actually pull it off is the question of the chapter.

One mystery number per shape, and the number isn't decoration: when time starts running again in Chapter 3, it will set how fast its shape breathes—tighter bends, bigger number, higher pitch. But first we need shapes that pass the audition at all. Think of the second derivative as a machine — feed it a shape, it hands back the yank-map — and we are hunting shapes the machine returns as scaled, flipped copies of themselves. Most candidates get mangled: a parabola flattens into a constant, a triangle wave shatters into spikes. Hunt below — and if you've taken calculus and are already shouting "the exponential!", hold the shout; it's about to be disqualified, and the disqualification is half the lesson.

One candidate passes immediately. Differentiate sin(kx) twice and you get −k² sin(kx): resized, flipped, but not redrawn — the yank-map is the shape. Every point gets pulled back in exact proportion to how far it strayed, so lockstep is granted: this shape can vibrate as one. Such shapes have names. Physicists call them modes — the cleanest motions a system owns. Mathematicians call the shape an eigenfunction of the second derivative and its scale factor (−k² here) the eigenvalue — a natural note of the operator, which can turn its volume up or down but cannot make it sing a different shape. And notice what just happened: a question about motion in time got answered by a condition on shape in space. The two axes will keep trading like this all essay.

Now, the exponential. The machine can't redraw ekx either — but look at its scale factor: +k², with a plus. Curvature pulls the sine back toward zero; it pushes the exponential onward. That isn't a vibration — it's a runaway: displacement breeding more displacement, the physics of explosions, not strings. Strike one. Strike two is more brutal: our stage is a finite stretch with both ends nailed to zero, and an exponential is never zero anywhere — even blends of growing and decaying exponentials manage at most one zero crossing, not the two we need. It can't get on the stage. The sine auditions perfectly: sin(nπx) hits zero at both ends for every whole number n, stays politely bounded, and comes back flipped instead of fed. Keeping your shape gets you noticed; fitting the stage gets you the job.

So the pinned string's modes are sin(nπx) — one arch, two arches, three — each carrying its number −(nπ)², whose minus sign and whose square are about to do an enormous amount of work. (The minus sign is exactly what the exponential didn't have.) These shapes are the string's vibes, in the most literal sense that word has ever had. The rest of this essay is learning to read them — and discovering they were never just about strings.

FINE PRINT · about that exponential

The machine's full roster of shape-keepers is a family of four: ekx, e−kx, sin(kx), and cos(kx) — the first two scaled by +k², the last two by −k². Boundary conditions are the bouncer: on a pinned interval, only the sines get in. Free or insulated ends would admit cosines; an infinite domain changes the guest list again. "Which wiggles?" is always answered by "which stage?"

And here is the best-kept secret of the whole roster: its two celebrities are the same celebrity. Feed the exponential an imaginary frequency and Euler's formula curls it into rotation: eikx = cos(kx) + i·sin(kx). The sine is an exponential wearing an imaginary number; the exponential is a sine that gave up on rotating. One family, photographed from two angles — Chapter 7's fine print finishes this thought.

Deepest of all: the natural stage for this whole subject is secretly a loop. Nail a string's ends down and you can mirror it — flip a copy upside-down past each wall, again and again — until it becomes one seamless repeating wave; pacing the interval back and forth is walking around a circle. (Chapter 6's fine print performs exactly this trick to repair the heat kernel.) And on a circle, the exponential's failure stops being a technicality: walk one full lap and ekx returns at the wrong height. It doesn't close. The only shapes that both keep their shape and close the loop are the ones that come back to themselves after a lap — which is practically the definition of a wiggle. Every 🔊 button in this essay is quietly making this argument: looping a shape in time is putting it on a circle.

Chapter 2

Every shape is a chord

A pure wiggle is a vibration photographed — frozen in space. (Run it through time instead and it's a pure tone; the 🔊 buttons in this essay are exactly that conversion.) The real claim — Fourier's claim, scandalous in 1807 — is that every reasonable shape on an interval is a chord: a sum of pure wiggles at frequencies 1, 2, 3, … each with its own volume.

Let that bother you for a second, because it bothered the greatest mathematicians alive in 1807. A chord is built from the politest ingredients imaginable: smooth, symmetric, endlessly repeating. Your scribble is none of those things — it has corners, lopsidedness, attitude. Fourier's claim is that politeness in sufficient quantity can impersonate anything. The committee reviewing his paper (Lagrange among them) did not believe it. The draw button below is the demonstration they didn't have.

f(x) = b₁ sin(πx) + b₂ sin(2πx) + b₃ sin(3πx) + ⋯ THE b's ARE THE FADER SETTINGS. THE SHAPE IS THE MIX.

Below is a mixing desk for testing the claim. A real mixing desk is the long console at the heart of a recording studio — one channel per sound source, and on each channel a fader: a sliding volume knob. This desk's ten channels are the first ten wiggles. (The π inside sin(nπx) is pure bookkeeping; it makes wiggle n fit exactly n arches into our interval.) Push the faders yourself, load a classic shape, or hit draw and sketch any curve with your finger or mouse — the desk will read off its fader settings and rebuild it.

⟡ PUZZLE — Load square, then triangle. Every even-numbered fader sits at exactly zero, both times. Why? (Fold each shape around x = ½ and look at its symmetry. Now fold sin(2πx) the same way.)

Two things to notice. First, jagged shapes (square, sawtooth) need lots of high-frequency wiggle: fader 9 is still working hard. Smooth shapes die off fast. Sharpness lives in the treble — to borrow audio's word for the high end of the frequency range (bass is the low end) — and the borrowing is the point: it's the same fact in both worlds.

Second — and this is the thesis of the whole essay — a machine that's blind to position can only move those faders one at a time. It can scale them, flip them, spin them; what it can never do is make them trade. So once a shape is written as a chord, the physics of any such machine stops being about the shape at all: it's choreography for faders that never listen to each other. Stop watching the curve; watch the board. Chapter 1 told us the second derivative is exactly such a machine. Time to point it at physics.

FINE PRINT · how the desk reads your faders

When you draw, the desk recovers each fader by computing bₙ = 2 ∫₀¹ f(x) sin(nπx) dx — once per fader. Why does that isolate exactly one wiggle's worth? Because pure wiggles are perpendicular: multiply two sines of different frequencies and the product's positive and negative lobes cancel exactly, integrating to zero. Multiply a sine by itself and the lobes can't cancel — everything is positive. So the integral against sin(nπx) is deaf to every wiggle except wiggle n. Orthogonality is the desk's solo button.

Why only sines, and no cosines? The ends of our interval are nailed to zero, and sin(nπx) is precisely the family of wiggles that respects the nails. Free the ends and cosines join the band; that choice of boundary conditions is what picks the instrument's legal wiggles.

One loose thread you can see on screen: load the square wave and notice the little horns that overshoot at each jump. They never go away, no matter how many wiggles you add — only get narrower. That's Gibbs's phenomenon, a genuinely strange fact about chords meeting cliffs, and a story for another day.

Chapter 3

Let go of the string

Chapter 1 froze the string at the instant of release; now unfreeze it. Newton says mass × acceleration = force, and the force on each bit of string is the curvature-pull we already met. Write the string's displacement as u(x, t) — how far the point at position x has strayed at time t — and Newton's sentence becomes the wave equation. (Subscripts count derivatives: utt is two in time — acceleration; uxx is two in space — curvature.)

utt = c²·uxx ACCELERATION = c² × CURVATURE. THE STRING IS PULLED TOWARD STRAIGHTNESS; c SETS HOW HARD.

As a statement about the whole string, this is hard: every point's future depends on its neighbors, who depend on theirs. So don't attack it directly. Instead, make the move this entire subject turns on — slowly, because it deserves ceremony. We don't know how the released shape will move; that's the question. But Chapter 2 guarantees that whatever the string looks like at each instant, it is some chord. So declare the format of the answer before knowing the answer: write u as a chord whose fader settings are unknowns, free to move in time.

u(x, t) = b₁(t) sin(πx) + b₂(t) sin(2πx) + b₃(t) sin(3πx) + ⋯ THE FADERS ARE NOW AUTOMATION LANES — UNKNOWN FUNCTIONS OF TIME

Read it twice, because it's a strangely confident thing to do. The mystery hasn't been solved; it has been relocated — out of the curve and into the knobs. One impossible question about an evolving shape has become a list of small questions about moving faders — in studio software, a fader that moves on its own is said to follow an automation lane, and our whole question is now just: what's drawn on each lane? And faders are easier prey, because of Chapter 1: the second derivative can't redraw a wiggle — it can only multiply wiggle n by −(nπ)², with c² tagging along. Feed the chord to both sides, match note against note, and the one equation falls apart into one tiny, private equation per fader:

bₙ″(t) = −c²n²π² · bₙ(t) EACH FADER'S ACCELERATION IS A NEGATIVE MULTIPLE OF ITS POSITION

Savor what that equation is. Second derivative equals a negative multiple of yourself — that is Chapter 1's lockstep condition again, verbatim, except the variable is now time instead of space. The condition that found our shapes has come back to govern their volumes. So each fader must itself wiggle: this is a simple harmonic oscillator — a mass on a spring, pulled toward zero with a force proportional to how far it strayed — and its motion is a sinusoid in time. Fader n doesn't fade; it oscillates, at a frequency proportional to n:

bₙ(t) = bₙ(0) · cos(nπct) NO FADER MOVES DOWN. EVERY FADER WOBBLES — FADER n WOBBLES n TIMES FASTER.

Which means the amplitudes of the wiggles are themselves wiggles. A standing wave, sin(nπx) · cos(nπct), is a wiggle in space married to a wiggle in time — and read the marriage contract closely: n appears in both factors. The spatial wiggle dictates the tempo of the temporal one. Tighter shapes ring faster; geometry sets the clock. This, finally, is the full answer to Chapter 1's question: a vibration is a shape the stage permits, breathing to a tempo the shape itself dictates. It's eigenfunctions all the way down. (The cosine, rather than some general phase, is just because we release the string from rest.)

Nothing is ever lost — energy just sloshes between shape and motion — and the frequencies come in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3 : …, which is why a plucked string sounds like a pitch with overtones rather than noise. The harmonic series is the eigenvalue list of a string. And c — the wave speed, set by tension and density — multiplies every mode's frequency by the same factor, which is why turning a tuning peg raises a string's pitch without changing its timbre. Constants tune the instrument; the equation's structure is the instrument.

FINE PRINT · the "falls apart" step, honestly

Here's the substitution the main text waved at. Write u(x,t) = Σ bₙ(t) sin(nπx) and feed it to both sides of utt = c²·uxx. The left side differentiates twice in time, touching only the b's: Σ bₙ″(t) sin(nπx). The right side differentiates twice in space, touching only the sines — and by Chapter 1, each sine just comes back scaled: Σ bₙ(t) · (−c²n²π²) sin(nπx).

So two chords are equal for all x. Chords match only if they match note by note (the orthogonality solo button again — integrate both sides against sin(nπx) and every other term vanishes). Therefore, for each n separately: bₙ″ = −c²n²π² bₙ. One partial differential equation in; infinitely many tiny, independent, ordinary ones out. That trade is the entire method — textbooks file it under separation of variables.

Draw your own pluck shape, then listen to it. The fader settings you loaded are the timbre: the color of a sound, what makes a piano and a trumpet playing the same pitch unmistakably different.

⟡ PREDICT FIRST — What should the wave equation do to one pure wiggle, alone? It's an eigenfunction — nothing can change its shape — so it can only stand there and throb. Draw your best sin(3πx) (three equal bumps, alternating up-down) and verify: a standing wave, fader 3 swinging solo.

So a released string never forgets. The shape you load is recited back, perfectly, once per period — initial conditions preserved forever in spinning faders. (That also dissolves Chapter 1's slow-motion footage: a pluck is many modes at once, each spinning at its own tempo, so the shape writhes as its chord perpetually rephases — and snaps back together the instant all the faders realign, once per period.) Hold that thought, and now do something perverse to the string: take away its inertia.

Chapter 4

Heat is a fade-out

A string rings because it has inertia: curvature drives acceleration, so the shape overshoots the flat line, swings past, and gets hauled back — forever. Now meet the medium with the same restoring instinct and no momentum to make music with. A temperature profile u(x, t) along a thin rod (ends held at zero) also wants to flatten — heat flows from hot to cold, every point drifting toward the average of its neighbors. But temperature has nothing to swing with: curvature drives its velocity directly. Replace Newton's two time-derivatives with one, and the wave equation becomes the heat equation:

ut = α·uxx RATE OF CHANGE IN TIME = (MATERIAL CONSTANT) × CURVATURE IN SPACE

Before doing anything clever, read the equation pointwise — it is unusually willing to be read. At the crest of a hot bump the profile curves downward — frowning — so uxx < 0, and the equation orders ut < 0: peaks cool. In a cold dip the profile smiles, and dips warm. Every point drifts toward the average of its neighbors, at a speed set by how bent its own neighborhood is. The equation is a field of little drift arrows — so let's just draw them:

Violet arrows: the equation itself — each point's velocity, α times its local curvature, always aimed at the average of its neighbors. (Arrow lengths are compressed to fit; the true contrast is more brutal.) The tight ripple is far more bent than the broad bump, so it gets yanked far harder — that is the n² hierarchy, drawn, before any algebra. And since a wiggle is nothing but peaks and dips, the arrows always oppose it: every mode is marked for decay. Watch the ripple vanish while the bump barely notices. And keep an eye on the lower strip: that's the rod itself, painted by its own temperature — the graph above is just its thermometer readout. The ripple is the mottling; diffusion is the mottling smoothing into one warm glow.

Now the clever part. Run Chapter 3's ceremony again — write u as a chord with unknown automation lanes, feed it to both sides, match note against note — and the equation falls apart exactly as before, except each fader's private equation is now first-order: its speed, not its acceleration, is a negative multiple of its position. That one change replaces every wobble with a slide:

bₙ′(t) = −α n²π² · bₙ(t)   ⟹   bₙ(t) = bₙ(0) · e−α n²π² t EVERY FADER SLIDES DOWN EXPONENTIALLY. FADER n SLIDES n² TIMES FASTER THAN FADER 1.

Stare at the time half of that result. bₙ′ = −(rate) · bₙ says my speed of decrease is proportional to my current size — and that sentence has only one possible consequence: exponential decay. It's the half-life law. Radioactive atoms obey it (each atom has a fixed chance of decaying per second, so you lose a fixed fraction per second). Hot coffee obeys it. The tail of a reverb obeys it — that's why studios quote a hall's RT60, the time to fall 60 dB: a half-life in audio clothing. Lose a fixed fraction per tick and you trace out e−rt: never linear, never quite zero, halving on a fixed schedule. Each mode of the rod is its own little isotope — and mode n's half-life is n² times shorter than mode 1's.

So that's the entire heat equation: a bank of half-lives, treble first — and not a little first. Mode 10 decays one hundred times faster than mode 1. Diffusion is a low-pass filter with a vendetta.

A word about α, the constant we owe the units. Curvature is measured per meter²; change, per second; α — the material's diffusivity, large for copper, tiny for styrofoam — is the exchange rate between them. But notice what α does and doesn't touch: it scales every mode's clock by the same factor. The material sets the tempo. The choreography — who fades relative to whom, the n² hierarchy — is pure geometry, and identical in copper and styrofoam.

Now the qualitative facts about heat stop being mysterious. Why does diffusion smooth things out? Because sharpness is high frequency (Chapter 2), and high frequencies are exactly what dies fastest. One equation remembers its initial shape forever and recites it back every period; the other forgets as fast as it can, treble first. You can diagnose which world you're in from the spectrum alone: faders spinning means waves, faders sliding down means diffusion. Same operator, same modes, same eigenvalues — only the clock attached to them differs.

Chapter 5

A field guide to fader choreography

Step back and look at the pattern. In goes a linear, position-blind equation; out comes a choreography — a rule telling every fader how to dance, each one alone, deaf to the others. The string said: spin, at tempo n. Heat said: slide down, at tempo n². The physics is in the dance, and once you can read the dance off the equation, you can start collecting equations like dance styles.

Here are four worth owning. Same rig as before, same initial shapes — only the law changes.

One rig, four laws utt = c²·uxx ut = α·uxx utt = c²·uxx − friction utt = −κ²·uxxxx

Press 🔊 with each law selected and keep the same shape loaded. Four laws, four sounds: spin is an organ-steady harmonic tone; fade is a tone dimming treble-first; spiral is a pluck that darkens as it dies — listen to any piano note and you'll hear exactly this; shimmer puts the partials at 1, 4, 9, 16… × the fundamental, and your ear immediately files it under bell.

The spiral deserves a word, because it's the sound of every real instrument. Add friction to the string — and a real string's losses (air drag, internal rubbing in the wire) bite harder at higher frequencies — and each fader both spins and slides, the slide fastest in the treble. The result: a note that doesn't just get quieter, it gets darker as it rings. That mellowing tail you hear in a held piano note is the spectrum of the string diffusing while it vibrates: heat and wave choreography running at once, in one fader bank.

The shimmer is stranger and just as real. A stiff bar resists bending rather than stretching, and the equation picks up two extra derivatives: utt = −κ²·uxxxx (κ for stiffness). Run the Chapter 1 move twice and each mode spins at tempo n² — so the partials land at 1, 4, 9, 16… times the fundamental. Not whole-number harmonics: inharmonic. That's why struck bars — glockenspiels, chimes — sound bell-like instead of stringy. And since real piano strings are slightly stiff, their partials run a little sharp of harmonic, which is why piano tuners stretch the octaves. An equation's eigenvalue list, audible in a tuning lever.

Two more moves this metaphor hands you for free. Resonance: so far every fader has merely coasted from its initial setting. But push a system periodically at one of its own natural-note frequencies and that one fader absorbs energy on every cycle and grows — a singer finding the wine glass's note, a bridge finding the wind's. It's also why small rooms boom at particular bass notes: a room is a 3-D version of our rod, with its own standing-wave modes, and a subwoofer near a room mode's frequency is feeding one fader relentlessly. Steady state: drive a system constantly instead, wait, and let every transient finish its fade. What's left — the part the operator can no longer move — is the steady state. Clamp a rod's ends at two different temperatures and every wiggle burns off, leaving a straight ramp: the shape with zero curvature, the one shape heat has no opinion about.

FINE PRINT · what "linear" is buying you

Everything in this essay leans on a second hypothesis we've been using without naming: linearity. A system is linear if doubling the input doubles the output, and the response to a sum is the sum of the responses. That second clause is the load-bearing one — it's the entire reason the faders never listen to each other. Feed in two modes at once and the system must respond as if each were alone; there is simply no mechanism by which mode 3 can hand energy to mode 7. No crosstalk, by law.

And you've heard the law break. Strike a cymbal gently: a few clean partials. Strike it hard and the sound blooms into that shimmering wash — energy cascading up the spectrum, modes colluding, new frequencies appearing that no fader started with. That's nonlinearity, audible. Same with ocean waves steepening into breakers, or a speaker driven into distortion (those "new harmonics" are the giveaway). The fader-choreography picture is the regime where amplitudes are small and the law holds exactly; push harder and the dance becomes a mosh pit. Linear isn't the whole world — it's the world's first-order truth, and an astonishing amount of it.

Notice how often the examples keep sneaking into rooms — room modes, reverb tails, RT60. Time to stop sneaking and go all the way in.

Chapter 6

The room is an instrument

Everything so far has run in one direction: take the input apart into wiggles, evolve each fader, reassemble. Call it the equalizer view — to understand what a system does, dissect what you feed it. But audio engineers have a complementary instinct, and it's worth feeling the gears shift as we switch to it: leave the input alone and interrogate the system. Hit it with the sharpest possible spike — once — and record what comes back. That recording is the system's impulse response. Mathematicians call it the Green's function, and they call the spike a delta function. Same object, different lab coats.

And the trick is not about rooms — it's about anything linear. Flick a wine glass: it answers at its own pitch. Knock on a wall: the thud tells you stud or hollow. Strike a bell, clap in a canyon, kick a car's suspension over a pothole. In every case the kick is the same dumb question — here is one sharp unit of everything; what do you make of it? — and the ring that comes back is the system's complete autobiography. Engineers do this for a living: tap testing, striking a bridge deck or an aircraft panel with an instrumented hammer and reading the structure's natural frequencies out of the ring. It is reading the room, for differential equations.

Why does one kick contain everything? Two answers, and we already own both. The bookkeeping answer is superposition: any input is a pile of spikes — one of height f(x₀) at every position x₀ — so if you know the response to one spike, slide it to each position, scale it, and add. That slide-scale-sum operation is convolution. The musical answer is better. A spike is the one signal that contains every frequency at full and equal strength — so a kick slams every fader to the top of the board at once, and then each mode simply performs its own choreography from Chapter 5. The ring-down you hear afterward is the eigenvalue list, played as a chord. A bell's strike tone is its modes sounding together; a hall's reverb tail is thousands of room modes, each decaying at its own rate. The balloon view and the equalizer view were never rivals: the pop is just every wiggle, asked simultaneously.

Now — is a metal rod "a room"? Almost, and the twist is worth being honest about. When you pop a balloon in a hall, the spike is a kick in time: the hall is the same hall at noon as at midnight, so you ask the sharpest possible question along the time axis. For heat, we'll ping in space: the spike is an initial condition — one single point heated white-hot — because a long rod doesn't care where you ask. The rule underneath both: deltas go along whichever axis the system can't tell apart. (You can also kick a rod sharply in time, or a hall at a point in space; a system has an impulse response along every uniform axis it owns.) FINE PRINT · the infinite rod, the walls, and why a gaussian

That tidy formula, with G depending only on the difference x − x₀, is the where-blind version: strictly, it's the law of an infinite rod. Our rod has nailed-down ends, and the demo shows you the consequence — bells squashing against the walls. The classical repair is charming: pretend the rod continues forever, but accompany every real tap with phantom mirror-image taps of opposite sign reflected beyond each wall, placed exactly so the temperature at the walls cancels to zero forever. This is the method of images, and it means the pinned rod's Green's function is just the infinite rod's gaussian plus an infinite hall of fading mirror-gaussians.

And why is the free-space kernel a gaussian at all? A pleasing self-referential reason: the kernel's fader settings at time t are e−n²π²t — which, read as a function of n, is itself a gaussian. And the gaussian is famously the one shape the wiggle-splitter sends to another gaussian: bell in space, bell in spectrum. Heat's impulse response is the fixed point of the very transform we've been using to understand it.

Now the literal room. Its Green's function is what it does to one sharp clap — which is why studio engineers really do walk into a hall, pop a balloon, and record the result. Convolve any dry signal — dry meaning recorded with no room and no effects on it — with that recording, and the signal comes out wrapped in the room. That is the entire secret of a convolution-reverb plugin: the solution formula above, sold as an audio effect.

Chapter 7 · Coda

Why these wiggles?

We opened on a question — what is a vibration? — and the answer has been assembling itself chapter by chapter: a shape the stage permits, moving to a clock the shape sets. One piece is still missing. Why are the shapes always sinusoids? What's so special about them? Why did the universe pick them?

It didn't — translation did. The operator d²/dx² has no idea where you are. Shift a function left or right, then differentiate: you get the same answer as differentiating first and shifting after. Any operator that's blind to position has to share its natural notes with the act of shifting itself — and the shapes that shifting cannot deform are exactly the sinusoids. Slide a sine wave and you get the same sine wave, just rephased. Sinusoids aren't special because of calculus; they're special because space is the same everywhere, and they are the shapes that symmetry protects.

FINE PRINT · the shift-a-sine wink, made honest

A careful reader will object: slide sin(kx) and you don't get sin(kx) back — you get sin(kx + φ), which is a blend of sin(kx) and cos(kx). Fair. The honest statement is that shifting preserves the two-dimensional family {sin(kx), cos(kx)}: it stirs the pair into each other but can never leak into a different frequency. The pair is the eigen-object.

The cleaner fix is to let the wiggles be complex: eikx = cos(kx) + i·sin(kx). Shift that and it comes back multiplied by a single number, eikΔ — a pure phase, a true eigenvalue. Complex exponentials are the genuine eigenfunctions of shifting; sines and cosines are their shadows on the real axis. Everything in this essay is the real-valued shadow of a tidier complex story, which is why professionals write Fourier theory in eikx from the start.

Which tells you exactly when to expect a different instrument. Put the problem on a drumhead — a disk, where the boundary is a circle and the symmetry is rotation — and the natural notes change costume: Bessel functions radially, sinusoids around the rim. On a sphere you get spherical harmonics, the shapes behind atomic orbitals. The wiggles change; the recipe never does: find the operator's natural notes, write your function in them, and watch each amplitude evolve on its own. A vibration was never a thing; it's a relationship — between a medium, its boundaries, and time. Change the stage and the vibes change with it. The vibes, it turns out, are local.

That's the whole story. Pure wiggles are the alphabet that constant-coefficient — that is, position-blind — differential equations are written in. The heat equation reads a shape's faders and slides them down, treble first. The wave equation reads the same faders and spins them, treble fastest. The Green's function is the system's autobiography, recordable with one balloon. And when the geometry changes, you don't abandon the method — you just learn the local dialect.

It's also why the Fourier transform follows scientists into every field they work in. It is, at bottom, a wiggle splitter: a change of coordinates into the one basis that position-blind operations cannot deform. And in those coordinates, hard things collapse into arithmetic. Differentiating becomes multiply each mode by −n². Convolution — Chapter 6's whole slide-scale-and-sum production — becomes multiply the two spectra, pointwise. Even sliding a function sideways becomes a phase twist on each mode, with no points actually moved. Any problem built from shift-invariant pieces — circuits, optics, image filters, crystallography, probability (adding independent random quantities convolves their distributions, which is multiplication in wiggle-land, which is where the bell curve comes from) — gets simpler in this basis. You don't reach for Fourier because waves are everywhere. You reach for it because sameness-everywhere is everywhere.

Everything here keeps going: Fourier transforms on infinite domains, Schrödinger's equation (whose faders spin at rates ∝ n², cousin to the stiff bar — that's quantum dispersion, a wave packet's partials drifting out of step), and the spectral theorem holding court above it all. But those are other essays.
